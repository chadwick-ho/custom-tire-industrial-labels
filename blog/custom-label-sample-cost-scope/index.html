Custom Label Sample Costs: Agree What the Trial Order Includes

Published November 4, 2026

One offer includes a few existing labels. Another includes a printed sample using your artwork. A third proposes a roll for an equipment trial. Calling all three a sample makes the prices look comparable when the deliverables may answer different questions.

Before comparing sample costs for tire or industrial labels, agree the question the sample must answer, the construction and artwork it represents, the physical format and the delivered quantity. Ask separately what is charged, whether shipping is included and what any later-order credit would depend on. No fee, free-sample entitlement or credit rule is established by this guide.

Choose the question before choosing the sample

An existing stock reference can help discuss appearance or material feel. It does not automatically represent your approved adhesive, print, dimensions or roll format. A custom printed sample can address the proposed artwork and construction within its documented scope. A machine trial needs enough appropriate material in a suitable format for the equipment owner's agreed procedure.

For a tire tread application, a neat printed piece on a desk does not settle contact with the actual rubber. For an equipment asset label, the intended cleaning and handling conditions can matter. Request the evidence needed for that use rather than assuming the most expensive sample is the most useful.

Put deliverables beside every cost line

Use a small sample-order record with the following fields. Leave a field unresolved when the supplier has not confirmed it.

  • Reference: construction code, artwork revision, size and sample identity.
  • Quantity and format: pieces by design, rolls or sheets, and any machine-specific requirements.
  • Purpose: appearance, data layout, applied-surface evaluation or a defined equipment trial.
  • Price scope: preparation, printed material, any agreed testing and shipping, with inclusions or exclusions explicit.
  • Timing and next step: prerequisites, proposed dispatch and what the buyer will review after receipt.

Avery's roll-sample guidance provides a concrete example of a manufacturer defining a small custom sample run and its line-item basis. Its prices and conditions are specific to that service; they do not describe CYJ's offer. The useful purchasing habit is to identify exactly what one sample line represents.

Confirm credit and repeat-sample conditions explicitly

If a supplier proposes crediting a sample charge against a later production order, ask what amount, order conditions and timing apply. Preserve the response with the sample quotation. An informal statement that the fee comes back later leaves the production buyer without a usable basis.

Also ask what happens if the buyer changes the size, adhesive, finish or artwork after reviewing the sample. A new sample may require different work. Conversely, a minor content correction may not justify repeating every physical evaluation. The responsible reviewers should agree which question needs to be checked again and ask for the revised cost and scope.

Keep sample approval separate from bulk authorization

Identify what the sample actually represents before signing it off. Record differences between the sample and the proposed bulk supply, including a substituted liner or a hand-applied sample where machine supply is intended. Resolve relevant differences instead of approving them accidentally through a generic signature.

The sample approval checklist covers the physical and content review. CYJ's sample policy makes availability, cost and timing project-dependent. A sample order does not itself authorize the bulk quantity or prove every long-term service condition.

Prepare a sample enquiry with the actual tire or equipment surface, approved artwork, dimensions, proposed construction, trial purpose and required format. Ask for an itemized sample scope before deciding which offer answers your purchasing question.
