Calibration Status Labels: What Belongs on the Sticker?

A status sticker should help a user find the right instrument record. It should not pretend to be the calibration certificate or make an unsupported accreditation claim.

Quick Answer: Use the Label as a Status Pointer

Choose the visible fields from your quality procedure: equipment ID, calibration date, due date or due criterion, current status and a record reference when space allows. The controlled record holds the actual results, limits, method and approvals. NIST material discussing calibration labeling identifies equipment ID, calibration date and cross-reference to the certificate in its NVLAP context; it also warns that an accreditation symbol must not imply approval of the equipment. Do not put such a symbol on a generic mockup or label without authorization.

A Composite Workshop Example

Imagine two similar pressure gauges beside one test rig. The technician has a service binder but only one gauge has a readable asset ID. The due month alone cannot tell which record belongs to which instrument. This is an illustrative scene, not a claim about a customer. A stable asset ID on the body, plus a status label that points to the controlled record, makes the handoff less ambiguous. If a gauge is out of service, use the site procedure to indicate that status rather than leaving an old valid-looking sticker in place.

Separate Fixed and Changing Fields

The asset ID usually stays with the instrument; the status and due information change. That suggests a durable ID label plus a replaceable status label in some workflows. Other organizations use a single controlled label. The buyer should decide the update method, who may print it, and whether each reprint requires a recorded approval. A printer can reproduce approved fields and numbering rules but cannot assign an equipment calibration interval or declare an instrument fit for use.

Place It Where It Can Be Read

A sticker on a curved gauge case, oily housing or frequently cleaned instrument needs a different placement review from one on a flat panel. Avoid the dial, controls, seal, ventilation and existing safety markings. Test the finished-size text under the actual lighting. If the surface is too small, use a short ID with a record lookup rather than squeezing the entire history into unreadable print. Ask for material and print durability information for the specific cleaning and handling conditions.

  • IDENTITY: stable instrument or asset ID.
  • STATUS: current state in plain language where the procedure requires it.
  • DATE: calibration and next due information only when approved by the owner.
  • TRACE: record or certificate reference when practical.
  • CONTROL: named authority for changes, replacement and withdrawal.

What to Send for a Quote

Provide the asset list format, sample text, minimum case area, surface photos, color coding rule, update frequency and whether labels are printed in-house or supplied preprinted. Confirm if the label needs variable fields or a write-on area. Proof one real-size sample on each instrument shape, then review legibility and removal without obscuring the equipment marking. The label artwork is an interface to a quality process, not evidence that calibration was performed.

FAQ

Questions Buyers Ask

Does a calibration sticker prove the equipment passed?

No. Verify the current controlled calibration record and the organization authorized status decision.

Can a generic label carry an accreditation logo?

Not without the specific authorization and scope required by the relevant accreditation body. Do not infer accreditation from sample artwork.
